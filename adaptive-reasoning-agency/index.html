Adaptive Reasoning Agency

The extent to which agents should adapt.

Leonardo Ranaldi, University of Edinburgh and University of Rome Tor Vergata. Based on a talk given in September 2026.

Language agents powered by LLMs can plan, use tools and complete tasks that would otherwise take hours of human work. Yet they remain brittle when past experience no longer reflects the situation they face. A chatbot that answers incorrectly can be asked again, while an agent may have already edited a file, called API, or changed a database. The question is how far past experience should guide future actions, and what evidence should prompt an agent to adapt.

This essay examines adaptation as a reasoning problem. An agent needs to recognise what should remain stable across contexts, revise its decisions when evidence changes, turn experience into reusable procedures, and anticipate failures before acting. Together, these capacities help explain when an agent should change its behaviour and when it should retain what already works.

At a glance

  • Adaptation is a decision. We study how agents can learn when their current way of acting should change.
  • Structure supports transfer. Quasi-symbolic abstractions make reasoning structures explicit while retaining the flexibility of natural language.
  • Agreement can be regulated. A learned policy helps models decide when to challenge, clarify or accept user-provided information.
  • Prediction exposes failure early. A learned world model allows agents to assess possible consequences before acting and detect when their expectations become unreliable.

The flight is booked and the return is gone

The request above is an ordinary one. The agent searches for available flights from Rome, selects one, completes the purchase, and the evaluation that accompanies most agent benchmarks confirms the task was completed. The failure becomes visible only when the itinerary is inspected after the run, where a booking that should have remained unchanged has been replaced.

Two distinct lessons are available from this episode. The first concerns the task and states which booking should have been made. The second concerns the procedure and states that, before changing any booking, the agent should establish which parts of the itinerary must be preserved. The first lesson repairs one run. The second changes how the agent works on later tasks, including tasks that share little surface with this one.

Today a developer inspects the failure and adds the check by hand. The research question is whether the agent can learn to make that change itself, which requires deciding what the experience shows, which part of the procedure needs attention, and whether the revision holds on another task.

Adaptation as a decision the agent should learn

An agent selects actions through a task policy. Adaptation introduces a second level of decision-making, where the agent determines whether its current way of acting still holds after a user pushes back, an action fails or the environment moves. Three regimes can be distinguished by where that second decision comes from.

Fixed rule

“If this happens, revise.”

The trigger and the response are defined in advance by the developer.

Adaptive policy

“Read the evidence, then decide.”

The response is chosen case by case, although the policy that chooses it stays fixed.

Learning to adapt

“Use last time to decide better.”

Experience improves the policy that makes those choices.

The object being learned in the third regime is an adaptation policy. A retained experience earns its place only if it improves later behaviour, which gives a concrete test for every stored lesson: whether it prevented an error, whether it led to a better outcome, and what it cost.

The remainder of this essay follows four capacities that this view requires. The agent first needs a representation of what matters in the context and how its elements relate. Given that representation, it must decide whether new evidence justifies a change. A change that helps should survive beyond the single case, and a consolidated procedure should eventually allow the agent to change before it gets something wrong.

I Represent

What can reasoning preserve across contexts?

Reasoning strategies do not always transfer across languages or models. This raises a fundamental question: what aspects of reasoning remain stable when the context changes? Our earlier studies provide evidence across different models, languages and representations.

Across models

A small model learns a complex capability from a larger one through distilled demonstrations. What transfers is the shape of the demonstration, that is, the order of the steps and the way the problem is posed and solved .

Across languages

A reasoning strategy that works in one language may become less reliable in another. Performance follows the surface of the prompt, which indicates that the procedure was attached to the words in which it was learned .

Across representations

Writing the reasoning as code makes the structure explicit and language-independent, and step-wise accuracy improves. A programme, however, must be complete, whereas most problems are only partly formalisable .

Checkable or flexible

Formal logic makes reasoning steps explicit and verifiable, but applying it to open-ended problems can be difficult. Natural language is more flexible, although its reasoning is harder to verify. Quasi-symbolic abstractions connect these approaches by representing roles and relations explicitly, which makes them checkable, while keeping their content in natural language. The representation therefore works in open settings without requiring a full translation into formal logic .

Content effect offers a direct test of whether explicit structure changes behaviour. We compare free-form reasoning with deductive prompting, with quasi-symbolic guidance (QuaSAR), and with Boethius, a schema-guided framework that separates the identification of the logical schema from its instantiation on the concrete predicates .

From the prompt to the activations

The distinction between content and structure also matters inside the model. An Abstractor aims to bring internal representations closer to the underlying reasoning pattern, reducing their dependence on the particular words used in a problem. This provides a way to study whether abstract reasoning can be recovered from ordinary inputs and carried across languages .

Open question

Is the structure we write the structure the model uses?

The term structure has been used here for two objects, the schema supplied in the prompt and the region reached by intervening on the activations. If the two align, one measure of structure would predict both what the model does and what it holds internally. Establishing this correspondence is the first open problem of the programme.

    II Revise

    Which evidence should change behaviour?

    An agent can fail in two opposite directions. It can yield to a confident source that is wrong, and it can resist a correction that is right. A single threshold cannot resolve both failures, since tightening it against one makes the other more frequent. Helpful adaptation therefore includes the ability to challenge a claim.

    We argue that sycophancy is a policy-level failure. Existing approaches often intervene on the generated response, leaving the decision of when to agree or disagree implicit. We model agreement as a choice among interaction strategies: challenging unsupported claims (counter), requesting additional information (clarify), or accepting a correction when supported by evidence (align) .

    Different evidence needs different checks

    In multi-agent settings, pressure can also come from other models. A contested answer may rely on a chain of claims that is difficult to evaluate from the final response alone. Dialectic oversight makes these claims explicit: experts present competing arguments, challenge each other's premises, and a judge evaluates which position is better supported . The appropriate verification procedure depends on the evidence. Retrieved passages can be compared, while formal reasoning can help assess whether proposed decisions satisfy explicit constraints, as explored in patient–trial matching .

    Open question

    Can revision follow the evidence?

    An explicit policy improves accuracy, and explicit arguments can be challenged. An important question remains: does the model revise its position because the evidence has changed, or simply because another argument was presented?

      III Consolidate

      How does an agent learn from experience?

      Storing an interaction retains what happened. It does not guarantee that the next decision improves. The difficulty of memory lies in retaining something that remains useful when the situation changes, which again depends on the form in which experience is kept.

      Consider a request from WebShop: “Find me a pair of black-and-blue sneakers that are good in rain weather, have puffy soles, and cost less than $90.” Each requirement needs evidence from the candidate product. A quasi-symbolic memory keeps the relations in explicit fields and lets language supply the current case.

      EVA: a trace becomes a procedure

      We introduce EVA (Evolving Agents), which consolidates trajectories into reusable problem-solving mechanisms through this representation. The Perceptor observes the environment and maps the trajectory into a quasi-symbolic abstraction. The Actor conditions its policy on that abstraction and selects a tool action. The Controller decides whether to act, rebuild the abstraction, retrieve an earlier procedure or recover. The three modules read and update the same state .

      A longer technical post, Evolving Agents, follows EVA and WEVA step by step.

      Open question

      Did memory improve the next decision?

      Task success measures the outcome of a whole interaction. It cannot isolate whether a stored lesson helped the agent choose. The proposed test replays the same decision state and changes only the memory it receives, comparing a relevant procedure, a random procedure and no memory.

        IV Anticipate

        Can the agent change before the error?

        EVA asks how past experience changes the next decision. WEVA (World-Model Evolving Agents) asks whether the decision can change before the consequence becomes irreversible. WEVA predicts the states induced by candidate actions, discards the branches that violate learned preconditions, executes the first step of the best surviving branch and compares the prediction with what is observed .

        The same procedure applies to the shopping task of the previous section.

        The signal changes before task success does

        The agreement between predicted and observed states provides a measure of model trust. When the environment changes, this signal can deteriorate before task performance declines, indicating that the agent's current expectations may no longer be reliable.

        The structured state is what makes the check possible. A structured predictive state reaches 92.8% against 87.3% for free-text prediction, with a logical error rate of 3.2% against 8.3%, since free text provides no fixed field against which an active precondition can be verified.

        A first step towards adaptation control

        In WEVA the signal is learned from experience, whereas the Controller that uses it is calibrated before interaction and fixed during it. A controlled toy experiment, separate from the EVA and WEVA measurements, makes the next step concrete. After a template change, a previously useful editing procedure becomes unsafe across 300 document tasks, and the controller chooses whether to act directly, retrieve a procedure or rebuild the task representation. Safe completion requires that the requested edit is completed and the protected documents are preserved.

        The extent to which an agent should adapt can now be stated as a choice of operation together with the computation spent on it.

        Open question

        Can the agent learn from the changes it makes?

        Representation, action and memory already learn from realised experience. The choice of when and how to adapt is learned before use and then held fixed. The next step is to let checked experience update that choice during interaction, through slow policy updates, verified outcomes and a stable comparison.

          What changes when procedures become shared?

          Individual memory captures what an agent has learned from its own experience. Team memory introduces a different problem: deciding which procedures can be safely shared and reused by other agents. With multiple contributors, shared knowledge requires evidence, provenance and mechanisms for resolving conflicts, updating outdated information and maintaining consistency.

          What transfers between the two backbones is the procedure, independently of the weights and of the wording. The benefit is largest where the work divides. EVA-M adds a planner and specialised executors that coordinate through one shared procedural memory, and on AppWorld it raises task goal completion from 51.2% to 57.8% and scenario goal completion from 36.8% to 43.5% with Qwen-2.5-7B .

          Reuse creates similarity

          Sharing a validated procedure avoids duplicated work and repeated failures. It also makes agents more alike. Agents are low-variance decision makers, and given the same context they take the same action, which turns an isolated poor decision into a correlated one. The relevant question is how much of a shared procedure should remain local, and that quantity is measurable.

          Evaluation has to look at the environment

          The opening example shows a broader challenge studied in WorldBench, which evaluates language agents on culturally grounded, multilingual workflows in realistic file-based environments. The benchmark examines both task completion and preservation of the surrounding workspace .

          Open question

          Can adaptation become collective and accountable?

          Three primitives are candidates, namely provenance (who wrote a procedure, and from what), dissent (a procedure for pressing private evidence) and cost (a price on an unfounded claim). Our contribution is an attribution, in which each primitive is tested against the specific failure it repairs.

            Which experience should change the next decision?

            This research treats adaptation as a reasoning problem. Structure tells the system what should remain invariant. Evidence tells it when to revise. Memory determines what persists, and prediction determines how far to act. Each capacity leaves one question that the existing results make testable.
